### ✅ q3 — "Quiz 3: Gauss's Law"
Four questions on Ch. 22, **all free response** (the default type — Claude grades the reasoning

**Q3 and Q4 carry a simulation** (`simulation: "gaussSurface"` on both questions,

as the charge moves inside, and drops to zero when the charge leaves: Gauss's law counted rather
than stated. The marker's $E$ changes the moment the charge moves, which is the distinction Q4
asks about (field yes, flux no). Physics in `src/quiz-sims.js` (`fieldLineCrossings`,
`pointChargeField`), tested in `src/quiz-sims.test.mjs`: an enclosed charge gives exactly one

count doubles with the charge, and the marker obeys Coulomb's law.

obvious candidates for `yesNo`, but that type posts a canned reply and never reads the student's
reasoning, which is the entire content of these questions ("Explain your reasoning"). A `choices`
question has the same problem and additionally makes the balloon question a coin flip. Free
response is the right fit: the verdict alone is worth nothing, the justification is the answer.

**Q4 is deliberately labelled (a)/(b)** so `detectParts` (`utils.js`) splits it into two graded
parts. The two halves have *opposite* answers — the field at a point on the surface **does** change
(the charge is now nearer some points and farther from others), while the total flux **does not**
(Gauss's law depends only on the enclosed charge) — and that contrast is exactly what the question
teaches. Splitting means a student who gets one and misses the other keeps the half they earned,
and the multipart grader prompts them by letter for the one still outstanding. The instructor's
source document asks the two as consecutive sentences with no labels; adding them is the only
wording change made to the source.

**Quiz text is plain text, not `MathText`.** Unlike homework prompts, a quiz question renders raw
(`ChatMessages.jsx`, the `msg.type === "question"` branch), so a `$q$` would reach students as
literal dollar signs. Q4 therefore writes the charge as a bare `q`. Keep `$…$` out of quiz text
until that renderer changes.

### ✅ q4 — "Quiz 4: Electric Potential"
Two questions on Ch. 23, **both free response**, for the same reason as `q3`: each opens on a
yes/no verdict, but what is being graded is the justification, and `yesNo` posts a canned reply
without reading it.

- **Q1 — zero-work path to the midpoint of a dipole.** Answer: **yes**. The plane through the
  midpoint perpendicular to the line joining the charges is a $V = 0$ equipotential (every point on
  it is equidistant from the two charges, so the two contributions cancel), and on that plane the
  field is everywhere perpendicular to the plane: the components parallel to the plane cancel by
  symmetry, leaving a field parallel to the dipole axis. Bring the test charge in from infinity
  along that plane and the electric force is perpendicular to the displacement at *every* step, so
  no work is done on any part of the path. The "in any part of the displacement" wording is the
  point of the question: $V = 0$ at both endpoints only guarantees zero *net* work, and a student
  who answers "yes, because the potential difference is zero" has not answered it.
- **Q2 — zero field does not mean zero potential.** Answer: **no**. $\vec E = -\nabla V$, so a
  vanishing field means $V$ is *constant* throughout the region, not zero; the constant is fixed by
  what lies outside the region and by the choice of reference point. The interior of a charged
  hollow conductor is the standard example ($E = 0$ inside, $V$ equal to the nonzero surface
  potential).

**Q2 is deliberately NOT split into (a)/(b)** the way `q3_4` is. Its two halves ("why or why not"
and "what can be said about the potential") have the same content: the reason the potential need
not be zero *is* that the field only fixes it up to a constant. Labelling them would grade one
idea twice and let a student earn half credit for restating the other half. `q3_4` splits because
its halves have opposite answers, which is a genuine two-part contrast.

**Wording changes from the source document.** The source writes the field as `\vec{E}` and
italicizes one word with `\textit{can}`. Quiz text renders raw (see the note under `q3`), so both
would reach students literally; the field is named in words and the emphasis dropped, per the
house no-emphasis style.

### ✅ q5 — "Quiz 5: Capacitance & Dielectrics"
Two questions on Ch. 24, **both free response**, for the same reason as `q3`/`q4`: Q1 says "explain
your reasoning" three times and Q2 asks for a mechanism, so the prose is the entire answer.

- **Q1 — two capacitors, same source voltage, one with twice the plate separation.** All three
  comparisons name the **same** capacitor, the one with the **smaller** separation:
  (a) $E = V/d$, so with $V$ fixed the narrower gap has the **stronger field** (by $2\times$);
  (b) $C = \epsilon_0 A/d$ and $Q = CV$, so the narrower gap has the larger capacitance and
  therefore the **greater charge** (by $2\times$);
  (c) $u = \tfrac12\epsilon_0E^2$, so the stronger field means the **greater energy density** (by
  $4\times$, since $u$ goes as $E^2$).
  The trap is (b): "bigger gap" intuitively suggests "holds more", but at fixed *voltage* it is the
  narrower gap that holds more charge. (At fixed *charge* the comparison would go the other way,
  which is why the "same voltage source" clause is load-bearing.) Worth knowing while grading:
  the **total** stored energy $U = \tfrac12CV^2$ differs by only $2\times$, not $4\times$, because
  the wider capacitor holds its lower density over twice the volume. The question asks for energy
  *density*, so $4\times$ is the answer, but a student reasoning via total energy is not confused,
  just answering a different question.
- **Q2 — fish freshness by capacitance.** $C = K\epsilon_0A/d$, so capacitance is directly
  proportional to the dielectric constant of whatever fills the gap. A fresh fish is mostly water
  ($K \approx 80$); as it ages it dries out and the water is replaced by air ($K \approx 1$), so
  its effective $K$ falls and **the measured capacitance drops**. High capacitance means fresh,
  low means stale. The full-credit idea is the chain "freshness → water content → dielectric
  constant → capacitance", not merely "water has a big K".

**Q1 is deliberately split (a)/(b)/(c)** so `detectParts` grades three parts. Unlike `q4_2` (whose
halves restate one idea), these rest on three different relations, and a student can easily get the
field right and then reason that the wider gap holds more charge. Splitting keeps the parts they
earned and makes the multipart grader prompt by letter for the one still missing. This is the first
PHY 215 quiz question with **three** parts; the engine is generic in `parts.length` (per-part credit
is `qPts / parts.length`, and completing all parts awards the question's full points directly, so
the 5/3 split introduces no rounding shortfall).

**Wording changes from the source document.** The source asks Q1's three comparisons as consecutive
unlabelled sentences with a single trailing "Explain your reasoning"; the labels (a)/(b)/(c) were
added (as in `q3_4`) and the instruction repeated per part. Q1's opening sentence was split in two
so the "same voltage source" condition is not buried in a subordinate clause. In Q2 the source's
`~` shorthand is written out as "about", and Q2 names the dielectric constant as a bare `K` for the
same reason `q3_4` writes a bare `q`: quiz text renders raw, so `$K$` would show literal dollar
signs.

### ✅ q6 — "Quiz 6: Current, Resistance, & Electromotive Force"
Three questions on Ch. 25, **all free response**, for the same reason as `q3`/`q4`/`q5`: Q1 and Q2
each open on a verdict ("is there a contradiction", "would it be appropriate") whose entire content
is the justification, and Q3 asks for a chain of reasoning outright. This is the first PHY 215 quiz
with three questions; `ptsPer` (utils.js) hands out whole points, so Q1 is worth **4** and Q2/Q3
**3** each (PHY 115's `q10` already has three questions, so this is new to the course, not the app).

- **Q1 — an electric field inside a conductor.** Answer: **no contradiction**. The week-1 result
  ($E = 0$ inside a conductor) is a statement about **electrostatic equilibrium**, where no charge
  is in net motion: any interior field would drive the free charges until their rearrangement
  cancelled it, so the equilibrium state is the field-free one. A conductor carrying a **steady
  current** is not in that state. Charge is continuously in motion, an external source of emf
  maintains a field along the conductor, and that field is exactly what keeps the drift going
  against collisions with the lattice: $E = \rho J$. The two statements describe two different
  situations, not the same one twice. Worth knowing while grading: the field inside a current-
  carrying wire is genuinely tiny compared with electrostatic fields (copper, 1 A in a 1 mm wire:
  $J = 1.3\times10^6$ A/m², $E = \rho J \approx 0.02$ V/m), which is why the electrostatic idealization
  survives; and a **perfect** conductor ($\rho = 0$) has $E = 0$ even while carrying current, which
  is the limiting case that makes the relationship clearest. A student who says only "one is
  static and one has current" has the right idea; full credit needs *why* equilibrium forces
  $E = 0$, or that the current requires a driving field.
- **Q2 — labelling a battery with its current.** Answer: **no, not appropriate.** Emf is a
  property of the battery itself (set by its chemistry), but current is not: it depends on what
  the battery is connected to, $I = \mathcal{E}/(R + r)$. The same 1.5 V AA delivers milliamps into
  a large resistance and amps into a small one, so a single current figure would be meaningless
  without naming the circuit. What **can** meaningfully be printed is a *capacity* (mA·h, how much
  charge it can supply before it is exhausted), a *maximum* or recommended current, or the
  internal resistance. Full credit is the reason (current is set by the external circuit, emf is
  not), not merely "no".
- **Q3 — why 24 V wiring weighs less than 12 V wiring.** For the same power, $P = VI$ means
  doubling the voltage **halves the current**. Wiring loss is $I^2R$, so at half the current the
  wire may have **four times** the resistance for the same loss, and $R = \rho L/A$ makes that a
  wire of **one quarter** the cross-sectional area, hence one quarter the copper mass (half the
  diameter). Thinner wires, less weight. The same $1/V^2$ scaling falls out of the voltage-drop
  criterion (allow the same *fractional* drop in the wiring: $R = fV/I \propto V^2$), so the answer
  does not depend on which design rule the student invokes. The key step is that it is the
  **current**, not the voltage, that sets how much copper is needed; a student who argues from
  $P = I^2R$ alone without connecting $I$ to $V$ at fixed power has not closed the loop.

**Nothing here is split into labelled parts.** Each question asks for one argument, so there is no
contrast to grade separately (compare `q3_4`, whose two halves have *opposite* answers, and `q5_1`,
whose three rest on three different relations). Q2's "why or why not" is the same idea as its
verdict, exactly the case argued under `q4_2`.

**Wording changes from the source document.** None beyond the symbol: the source writes the
resistivity definition as `\rho = E/J`, and quiz text renders raw (see the note under `q3`), so
LaTeX would reach students literally. It is written with the Unicode glyph ρ instead, which
displays correctly as plain text (PHY 115's `q6` does the same with fₛ and μₛ). All three question
texts are otherwise the source document's sentences verbatim.

### ✅ q7 — "Quiz 7: Direct-Current Circuits"
Two questions on Ch. 26, **both free response**, for the same reason as `q3`/`q4`/`q5`/`q6`: Q1 says
"explain your reasoning" and Q2 asks "why or why not", so the prose is the entire answer. Unlike
every earlier PHY 215 quiz, **both** questions are split into labelled parts.

- **Q1 — identical bulbs on a flashlight battery.** Let each bulb have resistance $R$ and the cell
  emf $\mathcal{E}$ with internal resistance $r$.
  (a) **Series: each bulb dims, and fast.** The bulbs add, so $I = \mathcal{E}/(NR + r)$ falls
  roughly as $1/N$, each bulb drops only $\mathcal{E}/N$, and per-bulb power
  $P = I^2R$ falls as $\mathbf{1/N^2}$ (verified: 0.375 W → 0.094 → 0.042 → 0.0038 W for
  $N = 1, 2, 3, 10$ at $\mathcal{E} = 1.5$ V, $R = 6\ \Omega$, $r = 0$).
  (b) **Parallel: each bulb is essentially unchanged.** Every bulb sits directly across the
  terminals, so with an ideal cell each keeps the full $\mathcal{E}$ and burns the same 0.375 W no
  matter how many are added. A *real* flashlight cell has internal resistance, and the rising total
  current drops more of the emf inside the battery
  ($V_\text{term} = \mathcal{E}R/(Nr + R)$), so the bulbs dim **gradually** — at $r = 0.35\ \Omega$,
  0.335 W at $N = 1$ down to 0.150 W at $N = 10$. Both answers are creditable; the contrast with
  (a) is the point, and a student who invokes internal resistance is reasoning better, not worse.
  (c) **The battery lasts longer in series.** Battery capacity is a fixed *charge*, so lifetime is
  set by the total current drawn. Series draws $\mathcal{E}/(NR)$, parallel draws
  $N\mathcal{E}/R$ — a factor of $N^2$ apart (checked: at $N = 10$ and $r = 0$, 80 h vs. 0.8 h for a
  2 A·h cell; still 63× with $r = 0.35\ \Omega$). The full-credit idea is that parallel bulbs each
  draw their own full current while series bulbs share one small one, not merely "series".
- **Q2 — capacitor, battery and resistor in series.**
  (a) **No, the resistor does not affect the maximum charge.** Charging stops when the current
  stops, and with $I = 0$ there is no drop across the resistor, so the whole emf ends up across the
  capacitor: $Q_\text{max} = C\mathcal{E}$, with no $R$ in it. (Verified: sweeping $R$ over three
  decades leaves $Q_\text{max}$ at 900 µC for $C = 100\ \mu$F, $\mathcal{E} = 9$ V.) The reason is
  the graded content — "no" alone is a coin flip.
  (b) **The resistor sets the rate.** It fixes the time constant $\tau = RC$ (0.01 s to 10 s across
  that same sweep) and limits the initial surge current to $\mathcal{E}/R$, which is what protects
  the battery and the circuit — without it the current is bounded only by the internal and wire
  resistance. So $R$ controls *how fast* the capacitor reaches its charge, never *how much*, which
  is exactly the pairing (a) and (b) are meant to draw out.

**Each question carries its own simulation** (added 2026-09-19). Q1 (`bulbsCircuit`,
`BulbsCircuitSim`): identical bulbs on a 3.0 V cell, Series/Parallel toggle, a bulb-count slider
(1–6) and an internal-resistance slider $r$ (0–1 Ω, opening at 0, the textbook's ideal cell);
the circuit is drawn with each bulb glowing in proportion to its power, scaled to a single bulb
on an ideal cell, and each bulb's current, voltage and power plus the battery current are printed.
Series bulbs visibly dim as $1/N^2$ (a), parallel bulbs hold their brightness at $r = 0$ and dim
only gradually once $r$ is raised (b, both answers the note above credits), and the battery
current reads $\mathcal{E}/NR$ against $N\mathcal{E}/R$ (c). Q2 (`rcCircuit`, `RCCircuitSim`):
$q(t)$ for $R$ (1–20 kΩ) and $C$ (2–12 μF) at $\mathcal{E} = 9.0$ V on fixed axes against a
dashed reference at $R = 5$ kΩ, with $C\mathcal{E}$ drawn as the line the curve approaches and
$\tau = RC$ marked on the curve, and a probe. Moving $R$ leaves the plateau where it is and
stretches the approach (a and b in one motion); moving $C$ is the control that does move the
plateau. Physics in `src/quiz-sims.js` (`bulbs`, `rcCharge`…), tested: one bulb is the same
circuit either way, series power falls as $1/N^2$ and parallel holds, energy balances including
$r I^2$, the final charge is $C\mathcal{E}$ for every $R$, $q(\tau) = 0.632\,C\mathcal{E}$,
and $i = dq/dt$.

**Both questions are split into labelled parts**, and each split earns its place the way `q5_1`'s
does rather than `q4_2`'s. Q1's (a) and (b) have **opposite** answers (dims sharply vs. essentially
unchanged) and (c) rests on a third relation (total current, not per-bulb power), so a student can
easily get one and miss another. Q2's two halves likewise have unrelated answers: $R$ is absent from
$Q_\text{max}$ but is the whole story for $\tau$. Q2's "why or why not" stays folded into (a) — it
is the justification of that same verdict, the case argued under `q4_2` — so the question is two
parts, not three. Q1 at 3 parts and Q2 at 2 both divide 5 points; completing every part awards the
question's full points directly (`App.jsx`), so neither split introduces a rounding shortfall.

**Wording changes from the source document.** The source asks Q1's brightness half as one question
answered "(i) in series and (ii) in parallel", with the battery-life question labelled (b). Those
are relabelled **(a)/(b)/(c)** here, and the relabel is **required, not stylistic**: `detectParts`
matches a single lowercase letter in parens, so "(i)" would have registered as a third part
alongside (a)/(b) while "(ii)" went unmatched — run on the source string verbatim it returns
`['a','i','b']`, splitting the question into the wrong three parts. "Explain your reasoning" is
repeated per part (as in `q5_1`). Q2's opening subordinate clause is promoted to its own sentence
so the series arrangement is stated before it is asked about, and its two questions are labelled
(a)/(b). No other change: both texts are otherwise the source document's sentences.

### ✅ q8 — "Quiz 8: Magnetic Field & Magnetic Forces"
Two questions on Ch. 27, **both free response**, for the same reason as `q3`–`q7`: Q1 asks "if
so, how? if not, why not?" and Q2 asks "how", so the prose is the entire answer. Q1 stays whole;
Q2 is split into labelled parts.

**Both questions carry a simulation** (`simulation: "chargeInB"` on both, `ChargeInBSim`, added
2026-09-19): a proton (or the same particle with the opposite charge) launched into a uniform
$\vec B$ along $+x$ at a chosen speed and angle $\theta$ to $\vec B$, animated in an oblique 3D
projection with $\vec v$ and $\vec F$ drawn at the particle and speed, kinetic energy, $|F|$ and
the radius read out. Along $\vec B$ ($\theta = 0$ or 180°) the path is a straight line and
$F = 0$ (Q1). Across it the path is a circle of radius $mv/|q|B$, at other angles a helix, and
the speed and $K$ readouts never move while the velocity arrow swings round and $\vec F$ stays
perpendicular to it (Q2a). Closed forms in `src/quiz-sims.js` (Y&F 27.11–27.12), tested: no
force and a straight line along $\vec B$, a circle of the right radius at constant speed across
it, $\vec v\cdot(\vec v\times\vec B) = 0$ all along a helix, and a negative charge circling
the other way.

- **Q1 — can a charged particle move through a magnetic field with no force on it?** Answer:
  **yes.** $\vec F = q\vec v\times\vec B$ has magnitude $|q|vB\sin\phi$, so the force vanishes
  whenever the velocity is **parallel or antiparallel to the field** ($\phi = 0°$ or $180°$); the
  particle then moves in a straight line along a field line at constant velocity, as if the field
  were not there. (Verified: at $\phi = 0°$ the force is exactly zero and at $180°$ it is
  $10^{-30}$ N, pure rounding, against $8.0\times10^{-14}$ N at $90°$ for a proton at $10^6$ m/s
  in 0.500 T.) A particle at rest also feels no force, since $v = 0$, but "move through" is asking
  for the parallel-velocity case, and the full-credit idea is the $\sin\phi$ dependence, not the
  bare "yes". A student who says "no, a magnetic field always exerts a force on a moving charge" has
  the common misconception the question is written to surface.
- **Q2 — a force that does no work still changes the motion.**
  (a) **The magnetic force is always perpendicular to the velocity**, so it changes the
  **direction** of $\vec v$ but never its magnitude: speed and kinetic energy are constant, which is
  exactly what "does no work" means, while the path bends, into a circle when $\vec v\perp\vec B$
  ($r = mv/|q|B$) or a helix otherwise. (Verified by integrating $m\,d\vec v/dt = q\vec v\times\vec B$
  over one full orbit: speed held to 1 part in $10^{14}$, the accumulated $\vec F\cdot d\vec r$ was
  $10^{-33}$ J against a kinetic energy of $8.4\times10^{-16}$ J, and the orbit radius matched
  $mv/qB$ to four figures.) The graded content is the perpendicularity argument; "it makes it go in
  a circle" without the *why* is half an answer.
  (b) **Yes, any centripetal force on a particle in uniform circular motion:** the tension in a
  string whirling a ball in a horizontal circle, the gravitational force on a satellite in a
  circular orbit, the normal force on a rider at the bottom of a loop, static friction on a car
  rounding a flat curve. More generally the **normal force** on a block sliding along any curved
  frictionless surface does no work (it is perpendicular to the displacement at every instant) yet
  it is what keeps the block on the surface. One correct example with the reason it does no work
  (perpendicular to the velocity) earns the part.

**Q1 is deliberately NOT split.** Its "if so, how" is the justification of its own verdict, the
same case argued under `q4_2`: the *how* (move along the field) is the *reason* the answer is yes,
so labelling them would grade one idea twice. **Q2 is split (a)/(b)** the way `q3_4` is, because its
two questions have unrelated answers: (a) is an argument about the magnetic force specifically,
(b) asks the student to generalize to other forces, and a student can easily give one without the
other. Two parts divide 5 points evenly; completing both awards the question's full points directly
(`App.jsx`), so the split introduces no rounding shortfall.

no labels; **(a)/(b)** are added so `detectParts` splits them. The source's "hoe can it have any

- **Q1 — two long parallel wires a distance $d$ apart, equal currents.** The stem no longer fixes
  the direction; each part names its own case. The parts were **reordered on 2026-09-19** from
  the source's (opposite: any cancellation? / far point as $d$ shrinks / how that changes with
  parallel currents) to lead with the parallel case, so the sitting starts where the simulation
  starts and then flips one current.
  (a) **Same direction: yes, on the line midway between the wires** (in the plane containing
  them). The part now reads "If so, where? Explain your answer. If not, why not?" (instructor's
  reword, 2026-09-19), so the reason is asked for outright rather than left to the grader's
  follow-up. With parallel currents the fields between the wires point in **opposite** directions and
  are equal in magnitude at the midpoint, so they cancel there, and nowhere else: off that line
  one wire is nearer, and off the plane the two vectors are mirror images whose perpendicular
  components now cancel while their parallel components add. (Verified: the net field is exactly
  zero at $(0, 0)$, nonzero at every other point checked in the plane and on the bisector plane,
  and at $r = 1$ m the far field is two wires' worth, $2\mu_0 I/2\pi r$, to four figures.) "Yes,
  at the midpoint between them, because the fields there are equal and opposite" is complete.
  (b) **Opposite directions: now no, nowhere (at any finite distance).** Each wire's field is
  $\mu_0 I/2\pi r$, circling the wire. **Between** the wires the two fields now point the **same**
  way (both into the page, say), so they add; at the midpoint the net field is twice one wire's.
  **Outside** the wires, in their plane, the fields do oppose, but the nearer wire is always the
  stronger, so they never cancel. Off the plane the magnitudes are equal only on the plane midway
  between the wires, and there the two vectors are mirror images whose components perpendicular
  to the wires' plane add rather than cancel. The net field vanishes only as $r \to \infty$.
  (Verified on a 1 m square grid at 5 mm spacing with $d = 2.0$ cm: the smallest field found was
  at the farthest corner; at the midpoint the field is $4.0\times10^{-4}$ T against
  $2.0\times10^{-4}$ T for one wire alone; on the bisector plane the field is purely
  perpendicular to the wires' plane at every height.) The common wrong answer is "still halfway
  between them", which is (a)'s answer carried over; the sim shows the zero crossing vanish the
  moment one current is flipped.
  (c) **Decreases.** At a point in the plane a distance $r$ from the opposed pair the two fields
  oppose with magnitudes $\mu_0 I/2\pi(r - d/2)$ and $\mu_0 I/2\pi(r + d/2)$, so the net field
  is $\frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi}\,\frac{d}{r^2 - d^2/4} \approx \frac{\mu_0 I d}{2\pi r^2}$ for
  $r \gg d$: it is **proportional to $d$**, and moving the wires closer together makes it smaller
  (it would vanish if they coincided). Note that it falls as $1/r^2$, not the single wire's $1/r$.
  (Verified at $r = 1$ m for $d = 4.0, 2.0, 1.0, 0.5$ cm: the exact two-wire field matches
  $\mu_0 I d/2\pi r^2$ to four figures and halves each time $d$ halves; at $d = 2.0$ cm it is
  50× weaker than one wire alone at the same distance. In the sim's units, $I = 3.0$ A and
  $x = 15$ cm, the probe reads $1.07\ \mu$T in magnitude at $d = 4.0$ cm and $0.27\ \mu$T at $d = 1.0$ cm.)
  "Decreases" with the reason (the fields cancel more completely as the wires approach) earns the
  part; the $d/r^2$ form is the full answer, not a requirement.
- **Q2 — twisted pairs.**
  (a) **Keeping them close together** makes the pair's stray field cancel: the two currents are
  equal and opposite, so at any point far compared with their separation the two fields nearly
  cancel, and by (1c) what is left is proportional to the separation $d$ and falls as $1/r^2$
  instead of $1/r$. The smaller the gap, the smaller the residual field on the surrounding
  circuitry. (Verified by Biot–Savart on a 2 m straight pair carrying 10 A, at 10 cm: one wire
  alone gives $1.9\times10^{-5}$ T, a pair 1.0 cm apart $2.0\times10^{-6}$ T, and a pair 2.0 mm
  apart $4.0\times10^{-7}$ T, in proportion to $d$.)
  (b) **Twisting** cancels the residual that closeness leaves. The leftover field of a close pair
  depends on which wire is nearer the point in question, so it reverses direction every half
  twist along the length: over any stretch longer than the twist pitch, successive half-twists
  contribute fields of alternating sign that cancel, leaving almost nothing outside the pair. A
  student may add that twisting also makes the pair's loop area essentially zero, so external
  changing fields induce no net emf in it (Ch. 29); that is a correct second reason but the
  question asks about the field the pair produces, and the alternating-sign argument is the
  full-credit idea. (Verified with the same Biot–Savart model: twisting the 1.0 cm pair at a
  10 cm pitch takes its field at 10 cm from $2.0\times10^{-6}$ T to $8.7\times10^{-8}$ T,
  and at a 2 cm pitch to $2.0\times10^{-8}$ T, a further 100× below the untwisted pair.)

  `hw7` 27.14's zeros had.
- **28.24's closing question and 28.31(a)'s second half are `text`, and each for a different
  reason.** 28.24's "Does this seem to be cause for worry?" is a judgment. 28.31(a)'s "Is the force
  attractive or repulsive?" is a two-way choice whose answer alone is a coin flip, the 27.42(a)
  treatment.
- **28.17(b) is a numeric blank followed by a `text` comparison, which the source does not ask for:
  see the departure note below.** The comparison half stays prose because "How does this field
  compare to …" is a comparison and not a value.
- **28.24's reference answer had to be reworded so a bare "No." passes** — see *A reference that
  leads with its justification demands one* below. This is the set's one genuinely new authoring
  lesson and it is now in the roadmap.
- **28.37, 28.74 and 28.79 are the symbolic core, seven `math` items between them.** 28.37 and
  28.74 are the same physics read off two different figures: two half-turns at a common center
  whose fields *oppose*, so the answer is a difference and not a sum. 28.79 is Ampère's law in
  three regions with a non-uniform enclosed current in the middle one. None of these has a number
  in it at all, so a numeric blank is not available even in principle.
- **28.15, 28.72 and 28.74 each split "magnitude and direction"** into a magnitude item (numeric,
  numeric and `math` respectively) plus a `text` direction, the `hw6` 26.71 / `hw7` 27.15
  treatment. In all three the direction is the reasoning half and the magnitude is the arithmetic.
- **Nothing in this set is ill-conditioned.** Every numeric is a clean product or quotient of the
  given data except 28.72, which is a difference of two terms ($108\ \mu\text{N}$ minus
  $28.0\ \mu\text{N}$); the subtrahend is a quarter of the minuend, so the result is nowhere near
  a cancellation and the ±2% band is meaningful.

#### 28.17(b) carries an instructor-added numeric blank (2026-09-23)

**This is a deliberate departure from the verbatim-wording rule, made on the instructor's explicit
instruction. Do not revert it.** The source's 28.17(b) reads only "How does this field compare to
one you would experience by being 5.0 cm from a long, straight household current of 10 A?", and it
was first authored as a single `text` part accordingly. Graded as prose alone, though, a student who
never computes the household field can still earn it by asserting "much larger": the number the part
turns on is never produced. The instructor's call was to make them calculate it, so the $40\ \mu$T is
now **its own numeric blank ahead of the comparison**, and the comparison keeps its own `text` item.

The added prompt is assembled entirely from the source's own words, so nothing in it is invented:
the verb is part (a)'s ("how large a magnetic field would you experience") and the clause is part
(b)'s ("by being 5.0 cm from a long, straight household current of 10 A"). Both halves keep the
source's letter (b), the same split convention as `hw7`'s 27.15(a) and this set's 28.31(a).

The comparison's reference was reworded at the same time: with the number now produced in its own
blank, it opens with the ratio and says outright that the comparison alone is a complete answer
(Authoring step 12). Verified afterwards: a bare "The lightning field is about 20 times stronger"
and a terse "20x bigger" both pass on every run, while "much bigger because you are closer" and
"about 100 times stronger" (the distance ratio alone) are both rejected; and on the numeric blank,
$4.0\times10^{-5}$ (teslas), $0.040$ (millitesla), $20$ (the ratio) and $800$ (part (a) reused) are
all rejected while a negative entry returns the free nudge.

**28.72's figure is the set's one real trap, and it is a figure-reading trap rather than a physics
one.** The $10.0$ cm dimension runs from the **wire** down to the bottom edge of the loop, not from
the loop's top edge, so the loop is $7.4$ cm tall and its two long edges sit at $0.026$ m and
$0.100$ m from the wire. Reading $10.0$ cm as the loop's height puts the far edge at $0.126$ m and
gives $85.5\ \mu\text{N}$ instead of $79.7\ \mu\text{N}$, which the ±2% band rejects. The figure was
re-read at 2× before the answer was committed, and the served crop shows the dimension arrow
clearly. Left as a trap rather than defused with a `freeHint`: reading a dimensioned figure is part
of what the problem tests, not a prerequisite to it. See the recommendation below.

**28.37 and 28.74's current senses were read off the figures, not assumed.** In 28.37 the $I_1$ lead
runs over the **top** of the circle from left to right (clockwise, field into the page at $P$) and
the $I_2$ lead around the **bottom** from left to right (counterclockwise, out of the page), which
is what makes the answer $\mu_0|I_1-I_2|/(4R)$ rather than $\mu_0(I_1+I_2)/(4R)$ and what makes it
vanish when the currents are equal. In 28.74 the arrow on the **inner** semicircle points left at
the top (counterclockwise, out of the page) and the one on the **outer** semicircle points right
(clockwise, into the page); since $a < b$ the inner term wins and the net field is out of the page.
In both, the straight leads and the short radial pieces lie along lines through $P$, so
$d\vec l\times\hat r = 0$ for them and they contribute nothing.

**Non-negative / angle tagging:** every numeric in the set carries `nonNegative: true`. All ten are
magnitudes, currents needed to produce a field, or a percentage, and not one of them can legitimately
come out negative. No item is `angle: true` (the set has no degree-valued blank).

#### A reference that leads with its justification demands one (found 2026-09-23)

28.24's closing part, "Does this seem to be cause for worry?", asks for a judgment and nothing more.
Its first reference answer read *"No. The line produces $3.8\times10^{-6}$ T at ground level, which
is only about 7.5% of the earth's own magnetic field…"* — correct, complete, and it caused the
grader to **reject a bare "No." four times out of five**, replying each time with "you've got the
right conclusion, but can you justify it?". The grader takes the shape of the reference as the shape
of the expected answer, so a reference that spends four sentences on a justification the source
never asked for turns an unasked-for justification into a requirement.

The fix was to say so inside the reference, not to shorten it: it now opens *"No. The question asks
for the judgment itself, so no on its own is a complete answer. What supports it is the comparison
already made above: …"*, and a bare "No." now passes on every run while "Yes, that is a dangerous
field strength" is still rejected. The reveal keeps its full reasoning, which is the point of
step 4 of the authoring procedure. This is now step 12 of
[../homework-roadmap.md](../homework-roadmap.md) § Authoring. `hw7`'s 27.29(c)(i)/(ii) are the same
shape and were re-checked against it.

## Modules — hand-built, so `MODULES_PHYSICS2` stays empty

The live PHY 215 class's 17 modules were built **by hand** in the instructor Modules editor. The
seed template is only copied into a class's RTDB `modules` node when that node is **absent**
(`App.jsx` ~line 396), so for this class the template can never apply. Populating
`MODULES_PHYSICS2` would therefore have no effect while silently diverging from what students
actually see — so it is deliberately left `[]`, with the `M(week, chapter, topic)` helper kept for
a future section created from scratch.

**Wiring new content into a module** (this is the required last step — the student's view is built
from modules, so a homework with no module item is invisible to them):

1. Instructor → Home → the module's ⋮ → **+ Quiz** or **+ Homework** → pick it → Add.
2. Set the due date on the item.

`+ Homework` was added to that menu as part of this week's work; before it, only quizzes could be
attached by hand, and homework items existed solely in seeded courses.

**Previewing what you attached:** click the item's **title** in the Modules editor and it opens the
real student screen in preview mode (badged "Preview", nothing saved, "← Back" returns to Modules).
That works for quizzes and homework alike, and is the intended way to proof newly authored content
such as `q3` before students see it. Homework preview writes no draft and no attempt counts, so you
can run the same set as many times as you like.

### Marking the source page break inside a stitched scan (2026-09-10)

Rebuilding a split example into one seamless image solved one problem and created another. **Y&F
sets a worked example in two columns**, so when the example runs over a page break the reading order
across the join is

> upper-**left** column → upper-**right** column → lower-left column → lower-right column

and with nothing between the fragments the two left columns butt together and read as one long
column. A student reading straight down the left side skips the upper right column entirely — which
in Ex. 22.3 means skipping the sentence that its own left column ends mid-way through. The
instructor hit this reading Lectures 1 and 2 and asked for the break to be marked.

**A dashed grey rule now runs across each such seam** (50% grey, ~0.5pt, 4.2pt dashes on 2.6pt gaps,
with ~6pt of air either side). It reads as a page boundary rather than as one of the notes' own
black `\secrule` section dividers, and it needs no caption: finish both columns above the rule
before reading on. It is drawn two ways, matched to print the same:

- **Lecture 1** stacks its fragments as separate PNGs in the `.tex`, so the rule is a macro there,
  `\scanbreak` (defined in that preamble next to `\lecfig`).
- **Lectures 2 and 3** stitch their fragments into one PNG, so the rule is baked at the seam row by
  `source/phy215/lectures/rebuild-split-scans.py`.

**NOT every stitched scan gets one, and this is the part that is easy to get wrong.** Some pairs are
not a page break at all — they are one tall page captured in two scrolls, where the lower left
column really does continue the upper one and a rule would be a lie. The test is the sentence at the
seam: *if the last line of the upper LEFT column runs into the first line of the lower LEFT column,
it is one page (no rule); if it runs into the upper RIGHT column instead, it is a page break (rule).*

#### Standing rule for every lecture built from here on

**A Y&F scan is a TWO-COLUMN page. Any time two fragments of one example are stacked — joined into a
single PNG *or* butted as separate images in the `.tex` — run the sentence test at the seam and, if
it is a page break, mark it.** This is not a Lectures 1–3 cleanup; it applies to every lecture in the
course, and it is the easiest thing in this whole set to get wrong, because a stitched scan with no
marker looks *finished*. The failure is silent and lands on the student, not on the build: nothing
overflows, no box is bad, the page just quietly loses a column.

Three shortcuts that make the test quick:

- **A `Continued` line in the upper fragment settles it** — that is Y&F saying "page break" out loud.
  (It is also page furniture the rebuild would normally strip, so read it before cutting it.)
- **A fragment whose right column is mostly white below a float is almost never a page bottom.** A
  real page fills both columns. That pattern (Ex. 22.1, Ex. 21.10 part 1) means a scroll split.
- **A seam that lands mid-sentence inside the left column is a scroll split**, full stop (Ex. 22.9).

Then match the existing geometry — `\scanbreak` for fragments that stay separate PNGs,
`rebuild-split-scans.py` for fragments that become one — so every lecture's rule prints the same.
